Two players. No talking. A square that shouldn't be fillable.

Charlie the referee locks Alice and Bob in separate rooms. He hands Alice one row of a 3×3 grid and Bob one column. Each fills their strip with +1s and −1s under a fixed rule, then the grid is revealed — and the two of them only win if they happened to write the same number in the cell where their row and column cross.

No hidden note, no signal, no peeking — just the numbers they choose. Play a few rounds honestly and see how close to certainty two separated people can actually get.

The strategy: before they're separated, Alice and Bob memorize the same fixed 3×3 table of +1s and −1s, built to satisfy the parity rule — each row's three numbers multiply to +1, each column's to −1. Whatever row Charlie hands Alice, she just reads it off the table; whatever column he hands Bob, same for him. No improvising, no signal needed once they've split up — it's all decided in advance. The catch: multiply all nine parity constraints together and you get a contradiction, so no fixed table can satisfy every row and every column at once. Whichever table they memorize, there's always at least one row/column pairing where it breaks — that's the one crack in the notepad no amount of clever pre-agreement can close.

Here you play it straight, as two people scribbling numbers with no communication — the classical version. Physicists have shown that no such pre-agreed strategy can beat 8 wins out of 9 in the long run; the only way past that ceiling is to replace the notepad with an entangled quantum state, which real experiments (including on quantum computers, in 2022) have confirmed actually works.

Same rules. Two entangled qubits instead of a notepad.

Charlie now also hands Alice and Bob one qubit each from two entangled pairs he just prepared. Instead of freely picking numbers, each of them builds a short quantum circuit — dragging gates onto their own two qubits — chosen to match their assigned row or column, then reads off what the measurement says.

They still can't talk, and Charlie still assigns rows and columns at random. Build the wrong circuit and the numbers are meaningless; build the right one and the win is guaranteed.

The strategy: before they're separated, Alice and Bob agree on nine short circuits each — one per possible row, one per possible column — each one measuring a different pair of quantum observables instead of reading numbers off a table. Whatever row Charlie hands Alice, she builds that row's circuit on her half of the entanglement; whatever column he hands Bob, same for him with his half. Neither knows the other's assignment, and no signal passes between them after the qubits are split — the correlation is already built into the entangled pairs Charlie prepared. Two people who've never compared notes since separating, producing numbers that always agree exactly: it looks like a psychic act. It isn't telepathy — it's linear algebra, and it's exactly the trick the Peres–Mermin square is built to prove no classical notepad can fake.
