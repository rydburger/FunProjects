The magic square on a noisy chip

On a perfect device the quantum strategy wins every game. The paper ran all nine row–column games on a Willow superconducting processor, 100,000 shots each, and won 98.30% of them. Here the same circuits run on a simulated chip with the error rates the paper reports, and you can turn each error source up or down.

2.3Where the losses come from

The paper's error budget (Fig. S3B) adds depolarizing noise after each gate plus readout bit flips, one source at a time, and finds it accounts for the measured loss. Below, our model does the same at the slider values above, next to the paper's numbers. The losses add up almost exactly because each one is small.

Why the two plans differ. In Plan I (the paper's main result) each player measures two observables at once and fills in the third with the product rule, so their own rule can never break. Only the shared cell can go wrong. In Plan II each player measures all three directly. Two are copied onto fresh ancilla qubits; the third is folded onto the player's second data qubit and read there, so each player reads three qubits. That means more gates and more readouts, and the referee now checks three things: Alice's row rule, Bob's column rule and the shared cell. The paper measured 97.73% for Plan I and 93.55% for Plan II in the supplement, and 98.30% for Plan I in the main text.
Exact noisy win rates come from back-propagating each game's scoring parity through the Clifford circuit (see quantum-games/nonlocal.js). Shot noise is sampled on top.
